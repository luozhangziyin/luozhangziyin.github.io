SmartCanteen — a WeChat MiniProgram for campus dining

Tsinghua University Freshmen Innovation Project · team of five · 智慧食堂

Tsinghua has so many canteens that deciding what to eat is a genuine problem — especially for a new international student, who may be reading a menu in a second language and has no way of knowing that a dish contains peanuts until it arrives. SmartCanteen is a WeChat MiniProgram that answers three questions in one place: what can I eat, where do I find it, and where can I sit?

The idea I find most interesting is that this is a two-sided problem. Diners cannot see what is available; canteens cannot see what is wanted. Both are caused by the same missing link — the data exists, but is never joined up. So the prototype serves diners with bilingual dish information, ingredient and allergen filtering, and live seating availability, while the same underlying data feeds an operator dashboard identifying over-production and unmet demand.

One design decision I would defend hardest: the system distinguishes "no allergen information available" from "verified to be free of this allergen." For anyone with a real allergy those are completely different statements, and conflating them is the kind of shortcut that makes a product dangerous rather than merely unhelpful.

The privacy design follows the same instinct. Seat occupancy is inferred from an overhead camera, but the question asked of the model is only "is this seat taken?" and never "who is sitting here?" — no facial recognition, raw frames processed and discarded at the edge, and only anonymous aggregates stored.

I worked on this in a team of five, building the WeChat MiniProgram in WeChat Dev Tools with JavaScript on the front end and Python for the detection and data side. The poster below shows the working prototype.

Cartesian motion planning and simulation for a multi-axis robotic arm

学生课外项目 — student extracurricular project

The aim was to let a robot arm be commanded in the coordinates a human actually cares about. The arm is a six-degree-of-freedom model — seven links and six joints, defined in URDF — simulated in Gazebo and driven through ros2_control. Using MoveIt 2 I converted Cartesian target poses into inverse-kinematics solutions for the joint chain, generated the trajectories between them, and verified the motion in simulation before it ever ran on hardware.

Configuring the arm meant working through the MoveIt Setup Assistant in full: the self-collision matrix, the planning group, the KDL kinematics plugin as the IK solver, and the two controllers that actually execute motion — a Joint Trajectory Controller on the robot side and a Follow Joint Trajectory controller on the MoveIt side.

I also benchmarked the planning pipelines rather than taking the default on trust, comparing OMPL, CHOMP, STOMP, TOMP and RRT-Connect on trajectory quality and planning time. The trade-off is real: the sampling-based planners return a path quickly but produce jerky motion that needs smoothing, while the optimisation-based ones give smooth paths at a computational cost that is hard to make real-time.

The part I am most pleased with is the TF work. The arm does not natively understand a point in space, so I used the TF library to carry a target from a depth camera's coordinate frame into the robot's base frame — which is what let this project link up with a separate student project using YOLO detection and a depth camera to locate objects. That collaboration is where most of my debugging time went: frames that disagreed with each other, and timestamps that did not line up.
